Balance of power is the oldest and most influential theory of international politics: the claim that states — particularly great powers — resist domination by any single state by forming coalitions, building arms and shifting alignments to restore equilibrium. It is also the theory that governments invoke whenever they describe their own foreign policy as "maintaining stability."

<KeyTakeaways>
- States balance against the strongest power, not the most threatening neighbour — power, not proximity, is the trigger.
- Balancing takes two forms: internal (rearmament, mobilisation) and external (alliances, alignments).
- The theory predicts that any bid for hegemony will eventually meet a coalition strong enough to stop it.
</KeyTakeaways>

## The core claim

The theory starts from a simple observation: in a world without a central authority, the state that can dominate everyone is a standing danger to everyone else. States therefore behave like scales: when one power grows heavy, others add weight to the other side — through arms buildups, alliances, or both. The result, in equilibrium, is a system in which no single state can impose its will, and the independence of all states is, paradoxically, protected by the rivalry of the great.

The classic formulation is attributed to the balance that European diplomacy maintained for centuries: no single power allowed to dominate the continent, coalitions formed and reformed to check whoever came closest. The Congress of Vienna system, the alliances of the two world wars and the bipolar standoff of the Cold War are all episodes in the same story.

## Two mechanisms

Balancing works through two channels. Internal balancing is a state strengthening itself: spending on defence, mobilising society, building the capabilities that make aggression costly. External balancing is coalition-building: seeking allies, signing treaties, creating structures that bind the potential hegemon's rivals together. Real foreign policies mix both, and the mix — more army or more allies — is the classic strategic choice.

A less obvious corollary is *bandwagoning*: states sometimes join the rising power rather than resist it. The theory treats this as the anomaly to explain, and historically the best predictor of which option states choose is geography and proximity — the neighbours of the rising power balance it first, while those farther away are freer to accommodate.

## Does it still apply?

Critics note that the theory struggles with nuclear weapons, ideology and the rise of non-state power. Its defenders answer that the logic survives the technology: alliances form around rising powers, coalitions oppose bids for regional hegemony, and states still treat the prospect of any single power's domination as the central problem of their existence.

<WhyItMatters>
Balance-of-power thinking is the default setting of foreign-policy establishments everywhere. When officials speak of "strategic equilibrium," "countering coercion" or "maintaining a favourable balance," they are running the theory in real time. To understand what states will do next, begin with what the balance requires.
</WhyItMatters>
