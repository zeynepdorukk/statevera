Deterrence and compellence are the two halves of coercive statecraft: using the threat of force to influence another state's decisions. The distinction matters because the two work on opposite logics — and confusing them is how coercive strategies fail.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Deterrence says: *do not start this*, and works by making the costs of action outweigh the benefits.
- Compellence says: *stop doing this* or *undo what you have done*, and works by raising the costs of continuing.
- Deterrence is easier: it defends the status quo. Compellence demands concession, and the target resists demands far more than restraints.
</KeyTakeaways>

## The two logics

Deterrence is the strategy of preventing: *if you attack, you will suffer consequences worse than any benefit*. It operates at the status quo — the potential aggressor decides whether to begin an action, and the deterrent's job is to make the decision obvious in advance. Because it asks nothing of the adversary except inaction, it is comparatively easy to establish and sustain. It does not require the adversary to admit anything or concede anything; it only requires the adversary to calculate.

Compellence is the strategy of reversing: *if you continue what you are doing, the costs will become unbearable — and if you stop, they will stop*. It asks the target to change course, to give up something, to acknowledge pressure. That is why compellence is intrinsically harder: the target loses face, domestic position and credibility by yielding, so it resists precisely because the demand exists. Every compellent threat invites the question of what happens if the target calls the bluff — which is why compellence is the strategy that most often escalates into actual force.

## Why the difference matters

The distinction explains several recurring phenomena. States that believe they are deterring discover they are actually compelling — and are surprised when the "deterrent" provokes rather than prevents. A purely defensive alliance posture deters; extending that posture to demand that another power dismantle its own arrangements compels, with all the costs that entails. Nuclear weapons deter superbly and compel terribly — a weapon that can destroy the world can stop an attack, but it cannot easily be used to force the evacuation of territory or the reversal of policy.

The practical lesson is the asymmetry at the heart of coercion: because the target of a compellent threat resists the demand itself, coercers must make the consequences of continuing so painful, and the path to relief so clear, that concession becomes preferable to defiance. If either element is missing — the pain or the off-ramp — compellence degenerates into stalemate, or worse, war.

<WhyItMatters>
Every debate about "credibility," "red lines" and "escalation" is, at bottom, a debate about the grammar of coercion: whether a given threat is a deterrent that stabilises the status quo or a compellent demand that tests it. Reading statecraft correctly requires knowing which sentence the great powers are speaking.
</WhyItMatters>
