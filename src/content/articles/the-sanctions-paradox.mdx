<KeyTakeaways>
- Sanctions reliably impose costs, but their primary effect is rarely the policy change they are announced to produce.
- The real impact of the sanctions era is structural: the world economy is quietly splitting into overlapping payment, technology and trade blocs.
- Targets adapt faster than sanctioners legislate — which is why the next generation of economic statecraft is about control of infrastructure, not lists of names.
</KeyTakeaways>

In theory, sanctions are the ideal instrument of a rules-based superpower: precise, reversible and non-violent. In practice, they are the foreign policy equivalent of a siege — slow, blunt and far better at inflicting damage than at producing compliance. The past years of unprecedented sanctions use offer a laboratory for what economic statecraft can and cannot do.

## What sanctions actually achieve

The record is not the failure narrative of critics, nor the success narrative of the Treasury Department's fact sheets. Sanctions on Russia were never going to produce a rapid policy reversal — the sanctions literature, such as it is, has never claimed that outcome for comprehensive packages. What they did do was degrade the target's war economy, raise the price of aggression and force a profound strategic reallocation of Russian resources. That is real leverage, with a specific limit: it does not translate automatically into political change.

The same pattern repeats across the case history. Sanctions on Iran changed the structure of Iranian trade and finance without changing Iranian enrichment policy — until a negotiated framework made sanctions relief the currency of diplomacy. Sanctions on Venezuela and North Korea have been maintained for years without achieving regime change. The instrument's comparative advantage is not conversion; it is coercion at the margin, and even then only when paired with a plausible diplomatic alternative.

<WhyItMatters>
The purpose of sanctions is often misread as changing a foreign government's mind. In practice their main function is changing its cost-benefit calculation, while signalling resolve at home. Policy makers who expect conversion will keep being disappointed; policy makers who design for constraint keep getting results.
</WhyItMatters>

## The structural consequences

The deeper story is what sustained sanctions do to the world economy. A target that cannot access dollar clearing, Western technology and Western insurance does not stop trading; it builds alternatives. Payment systems, logistics corridors, refining capacity and technology stacks are being duplicated outside the Western financial system at remarkable speed. The result is not the isolation of the target — it is the gradual emergence of parallel economic architecture.

This is the sanctions paradox: the more effective they are in the short term, the more they accelerate the long-term fragmentation that undermines their future effectiveness. Every sanctions regime is, in effect, a subsidy for the construction of a parallel system the sanctioner will later have to contend with.

<TheBigPicture>
Economic statecraft is entering its infrastructure phase. The next generation of leverage will be exercised not through lists of sanctioned persons, but through control of cables, ports, semiconductor fabs, refineries and the settlement rails of trade. The states that own the infrastructure of the world economy will set its rules.
</TheBigPicture>

## What the next phase looks like

The most consequential debates of the coming years are therefore not about adding names to lists, but about architecture. Whether the dollar retains its role as the default reserve and settlement currency is a political question as much as an economic one. Whether the world's technology supply chains bifurcate into two interoperable-but-distinct systems will determine the cost of the next confrontation before it even begins. And whether the "Global South" of the previous era becomes a swing constituency — trading with everyone, committing to no one — will set the terms of every future coalition.

<AnalysisSection title="The Facts">
- Comprehensive sanctions on Russia have removed its largest banks from dollar clearing and capped its access to Western technology, while its energy exports have been partially rerouted to non-Western buyers.
- Parallel payment systems and bilateral settlement arrangements have expanded among major non-Western economies.
- The U.S. Treasury has expanded secondary sanctions enforcement, extending the reach of its rules to third-country transactions.
- Estimates of the global trade that now flows outside the dollar-centric settlement system have risen sharply over five years.
</AnalysisSection>

<AnalysisSection title="What to Watch">
- The pace at which non-Western payment infrastructure reaches critical scale.
- Whether Europe and the United States can agree on enforcement of secondary sanctions without fracturing their own trade relationship.
- The re-export problem: whether advanced technology controls hold when production is spread across allied and neutral jurisdictions.
</AnalysisSection>

The sanctions era has changed the world more than it has changed most of its targets. That is not an argument against the instrument — in a world without consensus enforcement, it is often the only tool available. It is an argument for treating economic statecraft as what it is: a strategic engagement with consequences that outlast any individual campaign.
